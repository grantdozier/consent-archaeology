How this works, in plain English

The rest of this website is written like a spy movie, because that is the joke. This page is not. This page tells you exactly what happens to your information, in ordinary words, in the order it happens.

If you only read one thing, read Step 5. It is the part people are most surprised by.

First, the problem

There are companies called data brokers. Their business is collecting information about ordinary people and selling it.

They are not companies you signed up with. You have probably never heard of them. But many of them hold your name, the street you live on, the streets you used to live on, your phone number, your age, the names of your relatives, and a guess at how much money you make.

They sell that. A single record often goes for less than a penny.

The law in many places says you are allowed to ask them two things: what do you have on me, and delete it. There is a third question that is far more interesting, and almost nobody asks it. We will get to it in Step 4.

The short version

  1. You tell us your name and where you live.
  2. You prove the email address is really yours by clicking a link we send you.
  3. We check a list of data broker websites to see which ones have a record of you.
  4. We write letters for you — one per company — asking for your information back, and for proof you ever agreed to any of this.
  5. You send those letters yourself. We do not send them for you. This is deliberate, and Step 5 explains why.
  6. You keep a dated record of what you sent and what came back.

Step by step

1 You fill in a short form

We ask for your name, your email address, and your city and state. You can also add your phone number, addresses you used to live at, and the year you were born. Those extra ones are optional. They only exist to tell you apart from other people with the same name.

We never ask for your Social Security Number. There is no box for it. There never will be. Any website that asks for your Social Security Number while promising to protect your privacy should be closed immediately.

If you are worried about your Social Security Number specifically, the thing that actually helps is a credit freeze. It stops anyone from opening a new loan or credit card in your name. It is free by law, you can do it yourself in about ten minutes, and it protects you better than this website does. Start at annualcreditreport.com and freeze all three credit bureaus.

What happens to what you typed: it is scrambled before it is stored, so that even someone who broke into the database would see nonsense rather than your name. It is deleted automatically after 90 days if you do not come back.

2 We email you a link, and you click it

This step is boring and it is the most important thing on the website.

Before we look anyone up, you have to prove the email address you typed is actually yours. We send you a link. You click it. That is the whole test.

Why this matters: without it, anyone could type your name into this website and get a report on where you live. That would make this a tool for stalkers. The email link is the only thing standing between "a privacy tool" and "a way to find people." So there is no way to skip it, no password that gets around it, and no version for special users.

If the email does not arrive: check your spam or junk folder first. If it is not there after a few minutes, the service may be down — see the status box at the top of this page.

3 We check a list of data broker websites

We visit a fixed list of data broker websites and look for a record that matches you. The list is written down in the source code and anyone can read it.

We do not search the whole internet. We do not search social media, we do not read your email, and we do not look at anything that is not on that published list. If we ever add a company to the list, it has to come with the company's real legal name and business address.

For each company that has a record of you, we write down four things: the company's legal name, the address of its head office, its registration number if it has one, and a link to its opt-out page.

This part surprises people. These companies have street addresses and registration numbers. Several of them registered with the state voluntarily — in order to be allowed to sell information about you.

4 We write your letters

For each company, we can write up to four letters. You choose which ones. Each one is a real letter with your name at the bottom, quoting the actual law.

Tell me what you have
Asks the company to list every piece of information it holds about you.
Tell me who you sold it to
Asks which other companies they gave or sold your information to. This is how you find out how far it went.
Delete it
Asks them to delete your record. In several states they are required to, and they are not allowed to ask you why.
Prove I agreed ★ the important one
Asks them to produce the receipt. Not "do you have permission" — show me the proof. The date you supposedly agreed. The exact wording you were shown at the time. How they checked it was really you. And if they bought your information from someone else, who that was.

That last letter is the point of this entire website.

Under a European privacy law, and in a narrower way under several American state laws, a company that claims you agreed to something has to be able to demonstrate it. Not assert it. Demonstrate it.

Now think about how your information actually got where it is. A form you filled in years ago. A terms-of-service update nobody read. A company that got bought, where the customer list moved to the new owner but the paperwork did not. A list purchased from someone else entirely.

Many of these companies have your information and cannot produce the receipt. When they answer "we have no record of that," they have put it in writing — and that written answer is worth more than the information itself.

5 You send the letters. We do not.

This website has never sent a letter to a company, and it is not built to. It writes the letter and hands it to you. Sending it is your job.

People assume a website like this fires everything off automatically. This one does not, for two reasons.

The legal reason. When you ask a company for your own records, the company is required to check that the request really came from you. A letter arriving from some website on your behalf is a letter they are entitled to ignore, and many would. A letter from your own name and your own email address is one they have to deal with. Sending it yourself is not a limitation. It is the thing that makes it work.

The honest reason. If this website sent thousands of letters automatically, it would stop being thousands of people asking a fair question and start being one website generating junk mail. That is easy to dismiss and easy to shut down.

So how do you actually send it?

  1. Press the button to copy or download the letter.
  2. Fill in your mailing address where the letter asks for it. We do not collect your street address, so you add it yourself.
  3. Read it. It is your letter, going out under your name. Change anything you want.
  4. Paste it into an email to the company's privacy address, which is usually on the page we link to for that company. Send it from the same email address you verified in Step 2.
  5. Keep a copy of what you sent, and the date.

Some people prefer to send it on paper, by post, to the head office address we list. That is slower and it is harder to ignore. Either is fine.

6 You keep the record

Whatever comes back — including nothing at all — you can write down and keep. The date you sent it. What they said. Whether they answered in time.

Each entry is stamped with the date and locked to the entry before it, so the list cannot be quietly edited afterwards without it being obvious. You can download the whole thing at any time. It is yours.

Why bother? One person with one unanswered letter has a complaint. Ten thousand people with ten thousand dated, identical, unanswered letters have a pattern — and a pattern is the thing a regulator or a law firm can actually act on. You are not required to do anything with your record. You just have one, which most people never do.

Things this website will not do

  • It will not ask for your Social Security Number. No box, no "last four digits," no exceptions.
  • It will not promise that your information disappears. Nobody can make that happen, and anyone charging you for it is lying to you. We write demands and we keep track of who answers. That is the whole product.
  • It will not look up anyone but you. You can only get a report on an email address you have proved you control.
  • It will not give you legal advice. We are not lawyers and we do not represent you. These are self-help letters you send as yourself. If you want a lawyer, take your record to one.
  • It will not charge you. Everything works without paying. There is a donation button, it appears after you get your results, and if you dismiss it you will never be asked again.

What happens to your information

  • It is scrambled before it is stored. Someone reading the database directly would see nonsense.
  • It is never written into our error logs.
  • It is deleted automatically after 90 days of no activity.
  • You can download everything we hold and delete it immediately, at any time, without asking us.
  • The public counters on the front page are totals only. They never include a name, a city, or anything that could point back to a person.

Words you might not know

Data broker
A company whose business is collecting information about people and selling it, usually to other businesses. You did not sign up with them.
Consent
Permission. In privacy law it means you were told what would happen to your information and you agreed to it, knowingly.
Opt out
Telling a company to stop. Usually a form on their website, often deliberately hard to find.
GDPR
A European privacy law. It is the one that says a company must be able to prove you agreed, not merely claim it.
CCPA
California's privacy law. It gives California residents the right to see what a company holds, find out who they sold it to, and have it deleted.
Encrypted
Scrambled, so that it is unreadable to anyone without the key.
Arbitration
A private process, outside the courts, that most company terms require you to use instead of suing. It is usually written to favour the company — but when very large numbers of people use it at once, that stops being true.

If something goes wrong

If a page shows an error, the error is real. This website is deliberately built never to tell you something worked when it did not. If it says it could not reach the server, it could not reach the server.

The service status box at the top of this page checks the server right now, when you load the page. If it says the service is not running, nothing you submit will go anywhere, and the site will tell you so rather than pretending.
