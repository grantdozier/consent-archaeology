## 1. Why not a class action

Because the class action is already dead for almost every claim this project touches,
and the company killed it on page 40 of the terms of service.

Nearly every consumer ToS contains two clauses: (1) you agree to resolve disputes in
**binding individual arbitration**, and (2) you **waive the right to participate in a
class action**. The Supreme Court blessed this combination in *AT&T Mobility v.
Concepcion*, 563 U.S. 333 (2011), and extended it in *Epic Systems Corp. v. Lewis*
(2018). File a class action against a company with that clause, and the first motion
you see is a motion to compel arbitration. It usually wins.

This wasn't an accident. Individual arbitration was chosen by companies *because* a
$30 claim is irrational to arbitrate alone. The clause converts millions of small
injuries into zero litigation. For about a decade, it worked perfectly.

## 2. The judo: mass arbitration

Then plaintiffs' firms read the fee schedules.

Under the consumer rules of the major arbitration providers (AAA, JAMS), the
**company** pays the bulk of the arbitration costs — filing fees, case-management
fees, arbitrator compensation — often thousands of dollars **per case**, regardless of
outcome. That's the trade the company wrote: you give up court, they pick up the tab.

Mass arbitration takes the company at its word: **thousands of customers each file the
individual arbitration the contract demands, at the same time.** Each filing triggers
the company's per-case fees before any merits are decided. The clause built to make
claims individually irrational makes them collectively unignorable — and the company
cannot complain about the forum, because the company chose it. That is why the README
calls it the door they nailed shut from the inside.

## 3. Real examples (documented, citable)

- **DoorDash** — *Abernathy v. DoorDash, Inc.*, 438 F. Supp. 3d 1062 (N.D. Cal. 2020).
  Over 5,000 couriers filed individual arbitration demands. DoorDash — which had
  required arbitration — balked at the fees and tried to escape into court. Judge
  William Alsup compelled arbitration for 5,010 workers and left DoorDash facing
  roughly $9.5 million in administrative fees, writing that DoorDash was resorting to
  "the very device it denied to the workers" and concluding: *"This hypocrisy will not
  be blessed, at least by this order."*

- **Amazon** — after roughly **75,000** individual arbitration demands over Echo/Alexa
  voice-recording privacy, Amazon **removed the mandatory arbitration clause from its
  conditions of use entirely** (May 2021) — a company deleting its own favorite clause
  because it had become the liability. Widely reported (WSJ, June 2021); Amazon now
  litigates consumer disputes in court.

- **Samsung** — ~50,000 consumers filed arbitration demands under Illinois' Biometric
  Information Privacy Act. A federal district court initially ordered Samsung to pay
  ~$4 million in AAA fees. **Read the next section before treating this as a win.**

## 4. The honest part: what can go wrong

This tool would be lying to you — the thing it exists not to do — if it presented mass
arbitration as a slot machine. The mechanism is real; so are the failure modes.

**The Samsung reversal.** In *Wallrich v. Samsung Electronics America, Inc.*, 106
F.4th 609 (7th Cir. 2024), the Seventh Circuit **reversed** the fee order. The
claimants — tens of thousands of them — could not adequately **prove they each had an
arbitration agreement with Samsung**, and the court held fee disputes belong to the
arbitration provider, not a federal judge. Translation: if you cannot document that
you are actually a customer bound by the actual clause, the entire lever fails. This
is precisely why Consent Archaeology is obsessive about the evidence record — dated,
hash-chained, specific. Sloppy mass filings now lose.

**The rules have changed since the famous cases.** The AAA adopted Mass Arbitration
Supplementary Rules and a revised fee schedule (effective January 2024) that
dramatically reduce per-claimant initiation fees in mass filings and add flat fees and
process gatekeeping; JAMS followed with its own mass procedures. The eye-watering
per-case fee math from 2020–2022 no longer applies at the same scale. The pressure
mechanism still exists — it is simply smaller per claimant and more procedural.

**Companies redrafted their clauses.** Post-2021 ToS commonly add batching,
bellwether, and staged-resolution requirements, or route disputes to newer providers.
Courts sometimes strike the aggressive versions — the Ninth Circuit held Live Nation /
Ticketmaster's novel mass-arbitration protocol unconscionable in *Heckman v. Live
Nation Entertainment* (9th Cir. 2024) — but you should assume any modern clause was
drafted with mass arbitration in mind.

**What the individual claimant gives up and risks:**

- **You are still in arbitration.** No jury, minimal discovery, effectively no appeal,
  and the outcome is typically confidential and sets no precedent.
- **Individual recoveries are usually small**, and privacy harms are notoriously hard
  to value. Nobody should file expecting a payday; anyone who promises one is selling
  something. (We promise nothing. See the disclaimer.)
- **Costs and fee-shifting.** Consumer fee caps usually keep the claimant's provider
  fees low, but they are not zero; some clauses attempt loser-pays or sanction terms;
  counsel typically takes a contingency of any recovery; and a claim found frivolous
  can expose the claimant to costs.
- **Verification cuts both ways.** You will have to prove identity, purchase, or
  account — the same receipts we demand of controllers will be demanded of you.
- **Time.** Years, plausibly. Filing is the start, not the end.
